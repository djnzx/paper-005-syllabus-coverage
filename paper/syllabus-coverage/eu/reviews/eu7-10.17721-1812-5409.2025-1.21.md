# Review: Deep learning models for solving the problem of comparing unstructured textual information

**Authors:** Olena Skliarenko; Anatolii Pashko; Leonid Lytvynenko; Yanina
Kolodinska  
**Journal:** Bulletin of Taras Shevchenko National University of Kyiv. Series:
Physics and Mathematics, No. 1 (80), 2025, pp. 157–163  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.17721/1812-5409.2025/1.21

## 1. Problem

The authors target the automated comparison of large, unstructured, multilingual
legal documents — concretely, the Tax Code of Ukraine versus the German fiscal
code. These codes "share common principles but have a very different structure
and different sizes," which makes manual comparison impractical. The work is
motivated by Ukraine's EU accession, which requires harmonizing national
legislation with EU standards; Fig. 1 documents that the Ukrainian code received
constant amendments across 2010–2023, underscoring practical urgency.

The identified gap is that prior NLP work on legal text (court-document
processing in Golomoziy et al., 2023; Ianevych et al., 2023) "mainly focus on
classification, translation, and clustering," not on determining content
similarity between *pro-lingual* unstructured documents. A second, explicitly
named obstacle is the dependence of neural approaches on large prepared training
datasets.

The research question is therefore whether a **supervision-free** method, using
pre-trained multilingual sentence embeddings, can reliably surface semantically
corresponding articles across two fiscal codes written in different languages
and formats. The authors deliberately exclude generative models, aiming to
*supplement* rather than replace human legal analysis.

## 2. Research Tasks and Objectives

The stated objective is to develop an algorithm for determining the similarity
of multilingual texts via deep neural networks, then validate it on the
Ukraine–Germany fiscal-code pair. Concrete tasks (Section 1) are: analyze
document formats and convert them to data frames; define a comparison unit (a
single tax-code article); choose embeddings and a distance metric and pre-test
them on samples; iteratively visualize, clean, and re-run; and produce a final
correlation table linking similar articles.

Scope is bounded: the method is supervision-free, leaves generative models out,
and frames itself as a verifiable aid with a step-by-step replication guide for
other EU fiscal codes — not an autonomous legal-equivalence judge.

## 3. Methods

Pre-processing ("sub-deep" processing) dominates the pipeline. The Ukrainian
code, an HTML document with `<span class=rvts15>` section tags, was segmented
per article using regular expressions and stripped with the Python *
*BeautifulSoup** library; articles consisting of tables were dropped. The German
code, a PDF, was parsed with **PyMuPDF (fitz)**; because its table of contents
only "has visual resemblance" to a real ToC, the authors wrote a custom ToC
parser and fragment extractor, then used `get_text()`. Both were normalized into
JSON with similarly structured, similarly sized articles.

For similarity, they load Google's **Multilingual Universal Sentence Encoder** (
version 2 from TensorFlow Hub/Kaggle) and compute pairwise **cosine similarity**
via `sklearn.metrics.pairwise.cosine_similarity`. Results are rendered as *
*seaborn** heatmaps, initially filtered at `corr >= 0.30`. The unit of
comparison is one article.

The analysis is explicitly iterative. Early heatmaps showed strong
horizontal/vertical line patterns — evidence the model "picked up some trivial
textual similarities, but not the similarities in meanings." The authors
successively removed change-references and dates, article names (every Ukrainian
title contains "Стаття"), and numeric cross-references. When line patterns
persisted at full scale, they applied **Google Translate API** to render the
Ukrainian code into English, then raised the threshold to `corr >= 0.70`.

## 4. Key Results

The decisive empirical finding is qualitative but well-evidenced: only after
machine-translating both codes to a common language *and* tightening the
threshold to 0.70 did the spurious line patterns disappear, leaving "individual
dots" (Fig. 5) indicating genuine article-to-article correspondence rather than
lexical artifacts.

The output is a correlation table (Table 1) with concrete cosine values. For
example, the Ukrainian "Local taxes include…" article aligns with the German "
Non-personal taxes / Local jurisdiction…" article at **0.7**; other strong
matches include 0.72 (taxes/ancillary payments), 0.73 (definition of "
Taxpayer"), and 0.71 (claims arising from the tax debtor). These directly
satisfy the objective of producing an interpretable similarity mapping.

A reported negative result is instructive: despite the model not officially
listing Ukrainian support, initial untranslated tests looked promising yet
ultimately failed at scale, forcing the translation step. The authors note the
method can be inverted to highlight the *most distant* articles, flagging where
the two codes diverge.

## 5. Conclusion

The authors conclude they delivered a supervision-free, verifiable methodology
for comparing multilingual legislative documents, complete with a replication
guide transferable to other EU member states' fiscal codes, and emphasize the
importance of careful data cleanup and intermediate-result visualization. Future
work is the development of algorithms for standardizing the EU regulatory
framework.

One caution: the evaluation rests on visual heatmap inspection, hand-picked
thresholds (0.30 → 0.70), and manual spot-checks rather than any
precision/recall against a gold standard, so "similarity" remains qualitatively
judged; the document-specific cleanup and the reliance on paid translation also
limit straightforward replication.
