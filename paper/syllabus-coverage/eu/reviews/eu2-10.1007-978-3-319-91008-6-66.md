# Review: Adaptive Expert Systems Development for Cyber Attacks Recognition in Information Educational Systems on the Basis of Signs' Clustering

**Authors:** Valeriy Lakhno; Sergiy Zaitsev; Yuliia Tkach; Tetiana Petrenko
**Journal:** Advances in Intelligent Systems and Computing, Vol. 754 — Advances
in Computer Science for Engineering and Education (ICCSEEA 2018), Springer,
Cham, 2019
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-91008-6_66

---

## 1. Problem

The authors target a weakness of intrusion- and anomaly-detection systems
deployed in the information-educational environments of universities and
colleges. Such environments combine LMS portals, examination platforms, library
catalogues and BYOD student traffic; attack signatures drift quickly and
labelled incident data is scarce. Classical signature engines and even hybrid
neural-network / genetic-algorithm detectors trained on a fixed corpus lose
recognition quality once the attribute space of the protected system shifts —
the gap the authors name as motivation.

The research question is whether an *adaptive* expert system, able to retrain
itself on a moving stream of attack features and to tolerate noisy or
overlapping classes, can match hybrid neural-genetic detectors while remaining
interpretable. A second issue is the "error of the third kind" — picking the
right class label for the wrong attribute combination, which yields brittle
rules that collapse on the next batch.

## 2. Research Tasks and Objectives

The chapter formulates four tasks: (i) construct a model of an adaptive expert
system (AES) for the educational domain that ingests both statistical and
*remote* (distance-based) parameters of attack signs; (ii) define a clustering
procedure that partitions signs into classes amenable to fuzzy decision rules; (
iii) introduce an information-theoretic criterion that scores a partition of the
signs' space and drives the self-learning loop; (iv) demonstrate the approach on
real cyber-incident traces from university networks.

Out of scope are hardware-level countermeasures, cryptographic protocol
analysis, and forensic post-incident reconstruction; the work is confined to the
recognition stage of the defence pipeline.

## 3. Methods

The formal core is a clustering scheme over the matrix of attack
signs $X = \{x_{ij}\}$, where rows index observed events and columns index
features such as packet-rate deviations, failed-login bursts, URL-entropy and
protocol-mix anomalies. Partition quality is assessed with a modified
information criterion built on Shannon entropy and a Kullback–Leibler-style
divergence between empirical class distributions, i.e.
$$ J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k \, D_{KL}(p_k \,\|\, q) , $$
where $p_k$ is the within-cluster distribution of signs and $q$ the reference
distribution of the *a priori* classified training matrix. The AES retrains its
decision rules whenever $J$ degrades beyond a tolerance threshold.

Training proceeds in two phases. First, an *a priori* classified matrix supplies
seed decision rules; second, an on-line procedure updates centroids and
tolerance hyper-parallelepipeds, explicitly tracking third-kind errors so
ambiguous samples are quarantined rather than forced into a class. The
implementation is delivered as a software prototype, *Threat Analyzer*,
evaluated on traffic from several Ukrainian higher-education institutions.

## 4. Key Results

For the standard catalogue of attacks (port scans, brute-force SSH and web
logins, SQL injection, DoS, malformed-packet anomalies) the prototype reports
recognition rates of **76.5 %–99.1 %**, depending on attack class and traffic
stationarity. The authors explicitly state that these figures match the accuracy
of hybrid neural-network / genetic-algorithm baselines, while requiring fewer
labelled samples for re-training.

A second result concerns stability under feature-space drift: by clustering
signs first and only then fitting decision rules, the AES keeps recognition
quality above baseline when 10–15 % of the feature distribution shifts between
training and test windows — a regime in which neural detectors lose several
percentage points. Explicit third-kind-error accounting also reduces "right
label / wrong reason" classifications, treated by the authors as a precondition
for trustworthy self-learning.

## 5. Conclusion

The authors conclude that an adaptive expert system grounded in signs'
clustering and an information-theoretic partition criterion is a viable detector
for the heterogeneous, weakly-supervised environment of educational information
systems, offering accuracy comparable to hybrid AI baselines while remaining
interpretable and re-trainable on streaming data. Future work targets
encrypted-traffic side-channels, integration into a sectoral cybersecurity
decision-support system, and benchmarking against deep-learning detectors on
larger campus datasets.

A reader should note two caveats. The 76.5–99.1 % range is wide, and the chapter
does not disclose per-class confusion matrices or false-positive rates under
sustained adversarial drift; the practical operating point in a production SIEM
therefore stays uncertain. The "modified information criterion" is presented as
a self-learning trigger but its sensitivity to hyper-parameter choice and class
imbalance is left for follow-up papers in the same line of research.
