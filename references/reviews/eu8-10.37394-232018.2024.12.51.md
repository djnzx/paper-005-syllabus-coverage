# Review: Organization of Training with the Use of Digital Technologies for Ensuring Cybersecurity in the Educational Space

**Authors:** Leonid Arsenovych; Oleksandr Nikolaievsky; Olena Skliarenko; Leonid
Lytvynenko; Ivan Kydriavskyi  
**Journal:** WSEAS Transactions on Computer Research, Vol. 12, 2024, pp.
524–536  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.37394/232018.2024.12.51

## 1. Problem

The authors target the question of whether organizing instruction with digital
technologies measurably raises cybersecurity competence and a sense of safety in
the higher-education environment. The motivation is the rapid migration of
academic life onto digital platforms, which exposes students and staff to
threats such as phishing, cyberbullying, identity theft, and exposure to illegal
content.

They frame the gap explicitly: "one of the important unexplored questions on the
topic is assessing the cyber security situation in educational institutions
around the world, both in developed and developing countries." Prior work (e.g.,
Towhidi & Pridmore [7] on aligning curricula to industry; Eltahir & Ahmed [11]
on Sudanese HEIs) examines awareness or curricular adequacy but does not
experimentally measure the impact of a digital-technology-based course on
competence and trust.

The study draws on Kolb's four-stage experiential-learning model [3] (
experience, observation/reflection, abstract conceptualization, active
experimentation) to argue that cybersecurity cannot be taught by lectures alone,
motivating an interactive, tool-supported pedagogy whose effect is then tested
empirically.

## 2. Research Tasks and Objectives

Section 1.2–1.3 sets the aim — to study and evaluate the effectiveness of
digital-technology training for cybersecurity — and four concrete objectives: (
1) assess the digital competencies of both groups; (2) study students'
cybersecurity competencies at the start and after applying the pedagogical
conditions to the experimental group (EG); (3) study satisfaction with the
educational environment; and (4) find the relationship between cybersecurity
competencies and environment satisfaction.

The scope is a single-institution quasi-experiment with a self-report
questionnaire as the primary instrument; the authors explicitly acknowledge this
and sample representativeness as limitations.

## 3. Methods

The experiment ran at Drahomanov National Pedagogical University (Kyiv) with *
*241 students** in 2nd–3rd-year Pedagogical Education, drawn from **12 academic
groups** split into an experimental group (EG, n = 121) and control group (CG,
n = 120). It followed three stages (Table 1): summative (Feb 2023), formative (
Mar–Dec 2023, applying digital-technology conditions to EG vs traditional
methods to CG), and final (Jan 2024). The EG's digital conditions comprised
webinars/online platforms, simulation games, and interactive self-study courses.

Three instruments were used: the **Educational Environment Trust Scale (EETS)
** (University of Illinois, 18 statements), a 20-item cybersecurity-competence
questionnaire (Appendix A), and the **DigCompEdu Test** (63 questions across 6
digital-competence areas). Data were gathered via Google Forms and processed in
Microsoft Excel and **SPSS Statistics 18.0**.

Statistical analysis combined **Cronbach's alpha** for instrument reliability (
eq. 1), the **Mann-Whitney U-test** (eq. 2), the **Pearson correlation
coefficient** $r$ (eq. 3), a chi-square criterion (eq. 4), and t-tests, with a
0.05 significance threshold and reported effect sizes.

## 4. Key Results

At baseline the groups were equivalent: the Mann-Whitney U-test found no
statistically significant difference in digital competence, and the initial
cybersecurity questionnaire (Table 2) showed EG only marginally ahead (e.g., 90%
vs 85% on Q1) with non-significant p-values (mostly 0.05–0.32) — confirming "the
same initial conditions in both groups."

After the intervention (Table 3) the EG significantly outperformed the CG on
essentially every item: Q1 rose to 95% vs 85% (t = 3.2, p = 0.002, χ² = 10.2,
p = 0.001); Q2 reached 70% vs 50% (t = 4.1, p < 0.001, χ² = 16.8); Q9 reached
40% vs 25% (t = 3.8). On the EETS (Table 4) the EG scored higher on all five
scales — e.g., trust in teachers 4.5 vs 4.1 (p = 0.005), perceived teacher
support 4.6 vs 4.2 (p = 0.0025) — though the authors note effect sizes were "
small" and chi-square significance held for only three scales.

The correlation analysis (Table 5) showed consistently stronger ties in the EG:
perceived teacher support r = 0.63\*\*, trust in teachers 0.61\*\*, sense of
belonging 0.57\*\*, trust in peers 0.52\*\*, fairness of rules 0.48\*\* — all
exceeding the CG values (0.47, 0.45, 0.42, 0.38, 0.33).

## 5. Conclusion

The authors conclude that digital-technology-organized training significantly
increases cybersecurity competence and awareness and strengthens students' trust
in and belonging to the institution, recommending the results as a basis for
modernizing HEI cybersecurity curricula. Future work should test the
effectiveness of *specific* digital tools and programs.

One caution: the headline EETS table reports n = 20 per group rather than
121/120, an unexplained mismatch, and the baseline Table 2 lists nearly
identical Mann-Whitney U values (0.30/0.25) repeated across almost every
question — a uniformity that suggests the reported statistics may be
illustrative rather than independently computed, so the strong end-of-experiment
p-values should be read with care.
