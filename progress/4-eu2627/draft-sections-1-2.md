# Automated NIS2/ISO 27001 Curriculum Compliance Auditing Using Sentence Embeddings

*Draft — Sections 1–2 (Introduction, Related Works). Headline contribution: explicit-gap design.*

---

## 1. Introduction

The NIS2 Directive moved cybersecurity training from good practice to legal obligation. Since the transposition deadline of 17 October 2024, essential and important entities across eighteen sectors must show that their staff receive cybersecurity training aligned with the risk-management measures of Article 21. Failure carries fines of up to €10 million or 2% of global turnover, and personal liability for senior management. A training programme that merely exists is no longer enough; an organisation now has to demonstrate that its curriculum actually covers the competence areas the regulation expects.

Demonstrating coverage is harder than it sounds. A curriculum is written in the language of pedagogy — learning outcomes, modules, assessment criteria. A standard such as ISO/IEC 27001:2022 or the NIS2 measures is written in the language of control objectives and legal obligation. Auditing one against the other means reading both, deciding which learning outcome satisfies which clause, and recording where nothing does. Done by hand, this work is slow, depends on the individual auditor's interpretation, and rarely produces a record that a second auditor would reproduce. In Ukraine the task carries an extra difficulty, because curricula are frequently authored in Ukrainian while the reference standards circulate in English.

Recent natural language processing offers tools that look promising for this problem, yet none of them solves it directly. One body of work checks legal contracts — most prominently data processing agreements — against the GDPR, treating compliance as sentence-level classification (Amaral Cejas et al., 2023). A second body of work answers questions about regulatory text through retrieval-augmented generation, returning a passage and a generated explanation (Gokhan et al., 2024). Neither produces what an auditor of a training programme needs: a clause-by-clause statement of which regulatory requirements the curriculum covers and, more importantly, which it leaves uncovered.

This paper argues that a lightweight sentence-embedding pipeline can produce exactly that statement. Clause texts from the standard and learning-outcome texts from the curriculum are each mapped into a shared semantic vector space; cosine similarity then proposes matches, and a threshold separates covered clauses from uncovered ones. The approach reuses the text-comparison method that Skliarenko et al. (2025) applied to unstructured legal documents, and it draws its competence taxonomy from the cybersecurity training study of Arsenovych et al. (2024).

The contribution we place at the centre is not the matching itself but its inverse. Most compliance tools report what they found; ours is designed to report what is missing. We call this an *explicit-gap* design: every clause that receives no curricular match above the threshold is surfaced as a first-class output, rather than being absorbed into an aggregate score or a silent default. A single percentage of "coverage" hides the one uncovered requirement that an auditor most needs to see. By making the undefined region of the matching function visible, the method turns a coverage audit into an actionable gap list. The framing extends an earlier treatment of narrowed domains of definition as a route to software correctness (Rykhalskyy, 2025a), here applied to regulatory coverage rather than program semantics. This also keeps the tool honest about its own limits, because a clause the embeddings cannot match is shown, not guessed.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews four converging research threads and locates the gap. Section 3 states the problem and its relevance. Sections 4 and 5 define the goal, tasks, object, and subject. Section 6 details the method. Section 7 states the novelty, Section 8 demonstrates the pipeline on a worked NIS2 and ISO 27001 example, and Section 9 concludes with limitations and future work.

## 2. Related Works

Four research threads bear on automated curriculum compliance auditing: sentence-embedding methods, NLP-based compliance checking, retrieval-augmented generation over regulation, and domain-specific models for cybersecurity. Each contributes a component, and together they leave one problem open.

### 2.1 Sentence embeddings and semantic textual similarity

The technical foundation of our method is the sentence embedding — a fixed-length vector whose geometry reflects meaning. Reimers and Gurevych (2019) introduced Sentence-BERT, a siamese network that makes pairwise semantic comparison tractable by allowing cosine similarity over precomputed vectors. Gao et al. (2021) improved embedding quality with SimCSE, a contrastive objective later refined from two further perspectives by Xu et al. (2023). For multilingual settings, Feng et al. (2022) trained LaBSE across 109 languages, producing a shared space in which a Ukrainian sentence and its English counterpart land close together. The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark of Muennighoff et al. (2023) and the multi-functional BGE-M3 model of Chen et al. (2024) give practitioners a basis for choosing among the now-large family of encoders. The systematic review by Guder et al. (2026) surveys this evolution and confirms semantic textual similarity as a mature, well-characterised task. Skliarenko et al. (2025) applied precisely this class of models to the comparison of unstructured legal texts, and reported how a failure at one pipeline stage propagates downstream — a caution we carry into our own design.

### 2.2 NLP-based compliance checking

The work closest to ours treats compliance as a text-matching problem. Amaral Cejas et al. (2023) built an NLP system that checks data processing agreements against the GDPR, combining classifiers with cosine-similarity matching between agreement sentences and regulatory requirements. Their pipeline embeds each sentence and tests it against the rule set, the same mechanism we adopt. The requirements engineering community has since pushed toward generative models. Hassani et al. (2024) argued that compliance analysis should move from classification toward end-to-end strategies able to explain and justify their verdicts, and a companion study explored regulation analysis with large language models more broadly (Ilyas, 2024). LegiLM (2024) fine-tuned a legal language model specifically for data-compliance judgements, while an empirical study of food-safety regulation tested LLM classification of requirement-related provisions (2025). Most recently, the Compliance-to-Code approach (2025) reframed financial compliance checking as code generation. These systems are powerful, yet all of them target legal or contractual text and return a judgement rather than a coverage map of a training curriculum.

### 2.3 Retrieval-augmented generation over regulation

A parallel thread treats regulation as a knowledge base to be queried. Gokhan et al. (2024) released RIRAG together with the ObliQA dataset and an obligation-aware evaluation metric, framing the task as regulatory information retrieval and answer generation. RAGulator (2025) tuned retrieval for regulatory question answering, and Zheng et al. (2025) applied the same architecture to civil-aviation airworthiness regulations, which change often and so reward an updatable retrieval layer. Retrieval-augmented generation suits a user who asks a specific question and wants a grounded answer. It is a poor fit for the auditor who must verify that every clause of a standard is addressed, because question answering optimises recall for a single query rather than exhaustive coverage of a requirement set. Our explicit-gap design inverts that priority.

### 2.4 Domain-specific models and control mapping

A final thread adapts language technology to the cybersecurity domain itself. Aghaei et al. (2023) trained SecureBERT on a large cybersecurity corpus, and its successor SecureBERT 2.0 (2025) extended the model for threat-intelligence tasks; such encoders could replace a general-purpose model in our embedding stage when domain vocabulary dominates. On the mapping side, Nautiyal and Rashid (2024) proposed a framework for assessing an organisation's workforce knowledge against a standardised cybersecurity reference, which is conceptually adjacent to curriculum auditing though it stops short of an automated text-similarity method. Sargolzaei Javan (2025) built a machine-readable semantic model linking security mechanisms to ISO/IEC 27001:2022 controls, demonstrating the value of explicit control-to-evidence mappings. Semantic matching between heterogeneous model descriptions was earlier applied to decision-support architectures (Rykhalskyy, 2025b); the present work carries that idea from CAD and DSS models into regulatory clause text. The training context that motivates our work is established by Arsenovych et al. (2024), who studied digital-technology-supported cybersecurity training and supply the competence categories against which coverage is meaningful; the formal information-security-management framing comes from Lakhno et al. (2022).

### 2.5 The gap

Taken together, the literature supplies every ingredient yet assembles none of them for this purpose. Embedding methods give the comparison engine. Compliance-checking work shows that sentence-level matching against a requirement set is viable, but only for contracts. Retrieval-augmented systems handle regulatory text, but optimise for answering questions, not for proving coverage. Domain models and mapping frameworks address cybersecurity knowledge, yet none audits a training curriculum against NIS2 and ISO 27001 clause by clause, across languages, while surfacing the uncovered requirements as the primary result. That intersection — coverage auditing with an explicit-gap output — is the contribution of this paper.
